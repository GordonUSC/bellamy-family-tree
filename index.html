Where the ways cross

The book names one place. Thanking those who helped, Barbara writes: Thanks to our Waycross, GA family for all of your help. (page 68)

That tells us the family is in Waycross. It does not say who was born there, who moved, or when. So this map shows Waycross, and keeps the wider history of the era clearly separate.

Waycross and Ware County: where the ways cross

Ware County, in far southeast Georgia, was formed in 1824 from Appling County. Its seat, Waycross, was incorporated in 1874 where trails, roads, and later two major rail lines met, and the name is usually explained as the place where the ways crossed. The town grew as a railroad hub on the Plant System and Atlantic Coast Line. The lower part of the county forms a major part of the Okefenokee Swamp.

  • New Georgia Encyclopedia
  • City of Waycross
  • U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service
  • Wikipedia

Black Waycross: churches, doctors, and remembrance

Black Waycross built its own institutions early. The cornerstone of First African Baptist Church dates the congregation to 1870, and its church and parsonage, in a traditionally African American neighborhood, are on the National Register. The Okefenokee Heritage Center honors pioneers like Dr. G.P. Washington, the first Black doctor in Waycross, who opened the area's first Black hospital in 1890. Red Hill Cemetery, the city's oldest African American cemetery, holds about 2,000 burials.

  • National Park Service
  • Okefenokee Heritage Center

The times they lived through

The book records no birth years, so no one is placed on this line. These are the times the generations moved through, told with care, and sourced.

  1. 1863

    Emancipation and Reconstruction in Georgia

    1863-1871

    President Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation on January 1, 1863, and enslaved people had acted to secure their own liberty from the war's first days.

    Read more

    Georgia's Reconstruction ran from 1865 to 1871. More than 460,000 newly freed Georgians faced a new world with hope. In December 1865 the Georgia legislature ratified the Thirteenth Amendment, which ended slavery. Freedpeople quickly formed families, schools, and churches of their own.

    • National Archives
    • New Georgia Encyclopedia

  2. 1865

    Freedom to worship: independent Black churches

    1865-1870s

    After emancipation, African Americans built their own churches throughout the South, often joining Black denominations such as the AME Church.

    Read more

    Local communities paid for the buildings themselves. These churches became cornerstones of community life, serving as sites for worship, schools, and political meetings. In Georgia, Henry McNeal Turner traveled the state organizing AME congregations, and many ministers became civic leaders during Reconstruction.

    • Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture
    • New Georgia Encyclopedia

  6. 1910

    The Great Migration: choosing the warmth of other suns

    1910-1970

    Between 1910 and 1970, roughly 6 million Black southerners moved North and West in two waves, seeking jobs, dignity, and safety.

    Read more

    Georgia was a major source: 74,000 left between 1910 and 1920, and 260,000 more in the 1920s. Many Georgians settled in Detroit, Pittsburgh, and New York. Isabel Wilkerson's The Warmth of Other Suns tells it through people who left in search of a better life, deciding for themselves where to build it.

    • New Georgia Encyclopedia
    • Penguin Random House

  7. 1961

    Civil rights in Georgia: the Albany Movement

    1961-1962

    In southwest Georgia, the Albany Movement of 1961 and 1962 was the first mass movement of the modern civil rights era to aim at desegregating an entire community.

    Read more

    Local organizations, students, and SNCC workers led the way, and Martin Luther King Jr. joined in December 1961. Its mass meetings gave rise to the SNCC Freedom Singers, including Albany native Bernice Johnson Reagon, who carried church-rooted song to the nation.

    • New Georgia Encyclopedia

  8. 1976

    Family reunions: a homecoming tradition

    1865-present

    Black family reunions grew from the search for relatives separated by slavery, as freed people used newspapers, letters, and word of mouth to find one another.

    Read more

    Interest surged after Alex Haley's Roots was published in 1976 and became a hit television miniseries in 1977, sparking a national interest in genealogy. Today reunions, with their programs, photos, and T-shirts, are where family history is retold and the next generation learns where it comes from.

    • Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture
    • National Archives, Prologue blog

  9. 1977

    Othermothers, informal adoption, and wide circles of kin

    1970s scholarship

    Black families have long raised children in wide circles of care.

    Read more

    Sociologist Robert B. Hill's 1977 study for the National Urban League documented informal adoption, where relatives other than parents raise a child, as a strength of extended Black families. Patricia Hill Collins named othermothers: grandmothers, aunts, cousins, and neighbors who share mothering with bloodmothers. Scholars like Deidre Hill Butler describe nonblood relations as close-knit family across the African Diaspora.

    • ERIC, U.S. Department of Education
    • Wiley Blackwell Encyclopedia of Gender and Sexuality Studies
    • The Journal of Pan African Studies

  10. 1991

    Chosen family: kin by love and commitment

    1972-present

    Families are also made by choice.

    Read more

    Anthropologist Kath Weston's Families We Choose (Columbia University Press, 1991) showed lesbian and gay people building kinship that includes but goes beyond blood. In Black and Latinx ballroom culture, Houses led by Mothers and Fathers have served since the 1970s as chosen families, offering support, belonging, safety, and kinship. Chosen family is family, standing alongside the branches of any tree.

    • Encyclopedia.com
    • Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture
    • Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture
    • University of Michigan School of Information

Kin, care, and names

The book itself teaches this history: a Reverend and a Deacon at its root, children adopted in, a beloved elder listed simply as Big Mama, and branches marked Unknown that wait for a name.

The Black church as community anchor

For generations the Black church has been more than a place of worship. It was among the few institutions fully under Black control, so it became a school, a meeting hall, and a training ground for leaders. Ministers and congregations energized and sustained the Civil Rights Movement, and church buildings hosted the mass meetings, songs, and planning that carried it forward. Titles like Reverend and Deacon carried real community standing.

  • Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture
  • Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture

Big Mama: an honorific for the family matriarch

In many Black families, Big Mama is a title of love and respect for a grandmother or matriarch, the elder at the center of the family. Historian Clement Alexander Price wrote that his family called his grandmother's home Big Mama's House and passed that honorific name down to the children, a way of making an extended family feel rooted in one place. Sociologist Sandra Lynn Barnes has written about families asking, What would Big Mama do?

  • Forum Journal, National Trust for Historic Preservation (Project MUSE)
  • Cambridge University Press

How this page was made

Every person, partner and relationship comes from the book, with its page. Readings that are uncertain are marked. Nothing was added to the blood tree except the six people and partners Gordon added in 2026, each marked “added by Gordon, 2026”. Relatives the book marks in memory appear by full name, as the book gives them. Living relatives, and the people thanked in the book, appear by first name only. No birth dates, no addresses, no phone numbers, no photos of living people. Chosen family appear by first name. The art is symbolic: places, light and hands, never invented faces. The narrator is a narrator, not an ancestor's voice. History is from the National Archives, the Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture, the New Georgia Encyclopedia and other sources linked beside each passage.

A first version was built by Tasklet; this one keeps its care with sources and its 68-page index. Made by Darby for Gordon and the Bellamy family.
